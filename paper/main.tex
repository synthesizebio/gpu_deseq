\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\textbf{Motivation:} DESeq2 is a standard method for bulk RNA-seq differential
expression, but its iterative per-gene optimization makes repeated analyses
computationally expensive. We asked whether this structure could be mapped to
GPUs without changing the inferential workflow.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Results:} We developed \cudeseq, which combines gene-batched PyTorch
with CUDA-graph replay and a fused Triton dispersion kernel. Across six
real-data designs (7--300 samples, $P=2$--$5$), median p95 relative dispersion
error was \fact{8.5e-4}, significant-gene sets had Jaccard similarity of at
least \fact{0.99963}, and shrunken LFCs had Pearson correlation of at least
\fact{0.99722} relative to R~DESeq2~1.52.0. On one A100, the fastest mode was
\fact{3.6--35.1$\times$} faster than 12-worker R in end-to-end measurements. A
54{,}922-gene GTEx analysis with \fact{2{,}451} samples and $P=6$ completed in
a \fact{22.936-s} median, \fact{28.0$\times$} faster than the 12-worker R
endpoint. These results show that GPU execution can increase throughput while
preserving DESeq2-style inference across the evaluated scope.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Availability and implementation:} Open source under the MIT license at
\url{https://github.com/synthesizebio/gpu_deseq}; PyTorch and a CUDA GPU are
required for the reported acceleration.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Contact:} maxrhighsmith@gmail.com; greg@synthesize.bio\\[3pt]
\textbf{Supplementary information:} Appended to this manuscript.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Differential-expression analysis is a central step in interpreting RNA-seq
experiments. DESeq2 \citep{love2014deseq2} is widely used because it combines a
negative-binomial model of count variability with empirical-Bayes dispersion
estimation, outlier handling, multiple-testing correction, and stable effect-size
estimation. These features make it more than a gene-wise hypothesis test: the
pipeline shares information across genes while retaining gene-specific means,
dispersions, coefficients, and diagnostics.

This statistical structure also creates a distinctive computational problem.
For tens of thousands of genes, dispersion estimation, generalized
linear-model fitting, and log fold-change (LFC) shrinkage
\citep{zhu2019apeglm} repeatedly solve related but independent optimization
problems. R~DESeq2 executes this work on CPUs and distributes blocks of genes
among workers. The independence across genes suggests GPU parallelism, but a
faithful implementation cannot be obtained by replacing a single matrix
operation. Per-gene convergence, line searches, small design-matrix solves,
empirical-Bayes steps, and exceptional-gene fallbacks must all preserve the
reference method's statistical decisions (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic}).

Wall time is often acceptable when differential expression is performed only a
few times in an individual study. It becomes limiting when the analysis is
repeated across many cell states, perturbations, contrasts, or model outputs.
The bioinformatics community has recently seen growing interest in
virtual-cell systems and foundation models for gene-expression data
\citep{theodoris2023geneformer,cui2024scgpt,bunne2024virtualcell,
adduri2025state,koytiger2025gem}.
Differential expression is a natural evaluation endpoint for such models, yet
some recent benchmarks use rank-based tests on normalized expression as less
expensive surrogates \citep{roohani2025vcc,wei2026perturbench}. Accelerating
the established count model would allow statistical rigor and computational
throughput to be improved together rather than traded against one another.

\label{fig:schematic}

Existing efforts to accelerate negative-binomial differential expression have
largely been algorithmic or CPU-bound. glmGamPoi \citep{ahlmanneltze2020glmgampoi}
accelerates Gamma--Poisson GLM fitting on large, sparse count matrices. However,
replacing DESeq2 with glmGamPoi changes the inferential workflow and forgoes
DESeq2's automatic outlier replacement, limiting comparability with established
DESeq2 results and removing a safeguard against influential counts. PyDESeq2
\citep{muzellec2023pydeseq2}, a Python
reimplementation, improves interoperability and speed on large datasets, though by
its authors' own account it yields results ``similar, but not identical'' to
DESeq2; GPU acceleration has meanwhile transformed
adjacent single-cell workflows, where rapids-singlecell and ScaleSC report
$15$--$20\times$ end-to-end speedups \citep{hu2025scalesc}, but those gains come
from the dense and sparse linear-algebra core: PCA, neighbor graphs, and
clustering. Whether an iterative, empirically regularized workflow such as
DESeq2 can achieve comparable acceleration without materially altering its
outputs has therefore remained unresolved.

Here we test the hypothesis that DESeq2's gene axis can be treated as a batch
dimension while retaining its model, optimizer decisions, and downstream
inference. We introduce \cudeseq, which implements the complete supported
workflow in PyTorch \citep{paszke2019pytorch}, including count-outlier
replacement and apeGLM shrinkage. In addition to a general batched
implementation, we develop CUDA-graph \citep{nvidia2024cudagraphs} and Triton
\citep{tillet2019triton} dispersion paths that address launch overhead and
irregular convergence. We evaluate both numerical agreement and end-to-end
performance on published RNA-seq datasets, then use nested GTEx cohorts to
characterize scaling with sample count, design width, and GPU memory.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Reference workflow and supported scope}
\label{sec:pipeline}
DESeq2 \citep{love2014deseq2} models each gene independently with a
negative-binomial GLM. For gene $g$ with counts $K_{gi}$ over samples
$i=1,\dots,S$, design matrix $X\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times P}$, and size factors
$s_i$, it assumes $K_{gi}\sim\mathrm{NB}(\mu_{gi},\alpha_g)$ with
$\mu_{gi}=s_i\exp(x_i^{\!\top}\beta_g)$ and variance
$\mu_{gi}+\alpha_g\mu_{gi}^2$. Size-factor normalization accounts for library
depth; gene-wise dispersions are moderated toward a mean--dispersion trend;
negative-binomial GLMs estimate condition effects; Wald or likelihood-ratio
tests quantify evidence against the null; and independent filtering and
multiple-testing correction produce adjusted significance estimates. Optional
apeGLM shrinkage regularizes noisy LFCs while leaving the hypothesis-test
p-values unchanged. \cudeseq{} preserves this sequence and the information
passed between stages.

\subsubsection{Normalization}
\label{sec:normalization}
Median-of-ratios normalization is implemented with GPU tensor reductions and
quantiles. The default \texttt{sfType="ratio"} raises, as DESeq2 does, when
every gene contains a zero. DESeq2's alternative
\texttt{sfType="poscounts"} estimator is also implemented for such sparse
matrices.

\subsubsection{Dispersion estimation}
\label{sec:disp-overview}
Gene-wise dispersion fitting for both the MLE and MAP estimators
is batched across genes. The optimization methods
used for this stage are described in Sec.~\ref{sec:disp}.

\subsubsection{GLM fitting and hypothesis testing}
\label{sec:glm-overview}
Negative-binomial IRLS fits and Wald or likelihood-ratio tests are batched over
genes; the implementation details are given in Sec.~\ref{sec:glm}.

\subsubsection{Significance filtering and result assembly}
\label{sec:significance}
Cook's filtering, independent filtering, and multiple-testing adjustment are
implemented as vectorized array operations. For CUDA analyses, the robust
Cook's-distance calculation, thresholding, and count replacement operate on
the resident GPU matrices. Independent filtering obtains the rejection counts
for its 50 nested thresholds from one p-value ordering and performs the full
Benjamini--Hochberg adjustment only at the selected threshold. The standard
wrapper performs replacement and refitting when DESeq2's eligibility
conditions are met.

\subsubsection{LFC shrinkage}
\label{sec:apeglm-overview}
The apeGLM optimizer is batched across genes on the GPU; Sec.~\ref{sec:apeglm}
describes the implementation.

\subsection{Dispersion acceleration}
\label{sec:disp}
The dispersion stage is a batched port of DESeq2's \texttt{fitDisp} and the supporting
\texttt{estimateDispersions} logic. Its gene-wise MLE maximizes the
Cox--Reid adjusted profile log-likelihood
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\mathrm{CR}}(\alpha_g)=\sum_{i=1}^{S}\log \mathrm{NB}\!\bigl(K_{gi};\mu_{gi},\alpha_g\bigr)
\;-\;\tfrac12\log\det\!\bigl(X^{\!\top} W_g X\bigr),
\label{eq:cr}
\end{equation}
where $W_g=\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\mu_{gi}/(1+\alpha_g\mu_{gi})\bigr)$.
We retain DESeq2's initialization, line search, trend fit, MAP shrinkage, and
outlier rule. Because dispersion is a significant source of pipeline compute
time, we expand optimization beyond basic batching and provide three
alternative implementations of the gradient-ascent loop: eager, CUDA-graph,
and Triton.
The three implementations use the same optimizer decisions
and differ only in how those evaluations are scheduled.

\caption{The three execution modes for the dispersion loop.}

\paragraph{CUDA-graph chunked replay.}
\label{sec:graph}
In eager mode each gradient-ascent iteration launches a handful of tiny kernels
over $1$--$10$\,MB arrays, so the loop is dominated by launch latency.
Capturing the loop as a CUDA graph amortizes that cost
into a single replay, but two obstacles must be overcome.

The first is early exit. A fixed full-length capture would force every gene to
the maximum iteration count, which wastes the typical $15$--$40$-iteration case.
To prevent this we capture a $K{=}10$-iteration graph that reads and writes a set of
persistent state buffers in place, and replay it in a loop with a convergence
check between chunks. A chunk always runs to its boundary, so a gene that
converges partway through executes further iterations. We selected ten
iterations per replay empirically to balance replay overhead against excess
iterations after convergence.

The second is cuSOLVER. The Cox--Reid term needs $\log\det(b)$ and
$\operatorname{tr}(b^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}b)$ with $b=X^{\!\top}WX$, but
\texttt{slogdet}/\texttt{solve} host-synchronize and cannot be captured. Since $b$
is symmetric positive-definite for full-rank $X$, we replace them with an unrolled
no-pivot LU (\texttt{\_logdet\_and\_tr\_binv\_db}) returning both quantities from
elementwise and indexing operations alone, matching the \texttt{linalg} path to
$\sim$1e-15 for $P=2\dots6$.

Before capture the chunk is run three times on a side stream, warming the caching
allocator and any lazy kernel initialization. Captured graphs are cached on $G$,
$S$, $P$, the chunk length $K$, whether a prior term is enabled, the dtype and the
device. Only \emph{whether} there is a prior is selected on the device, because the prior
variance lives in the buffer set as a scalar tensor the replay reads, so one graph
serves datasets whose prior variances differ.
Because the graph uses the same routine,
it retains bit-identical parity with the eager mode implementation.

\paragraph{Triton full-loop fusion.}
\label{sec:triton}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/triton_dispersion_schematic.pdf}
\caption{Fused Triton execution of dispersion estimation. The same kernel is
invoked for the Cox--Reid MLE and the prior-regularized MAP estimate on either
side of the shared trend fit. Each gene maps to one program, which loads
$\mathbf{y}_g$, $\boldsymbol{\mu}_g$, and $\mathbf{X}$ once and retains the
objective, gradient, Armijo update, and convergence state inside the kernel.
Supplementary Algorithms~\ref{alg:triton-overview}--\ref{alg:triton-detail}
provide pseudocode.}
\label{fig:triton}
\end{figure}

Each Triton dispersion fit uses one kernel launch over a grid with one program
instance per gene (Fig.~\ref{fig:triton}). Each instance loads its counts, $\mu$,
and the design columns once into registers, then runs the iterative gradient-ascent
loop register-resident: the
Cox--Reid $P\times P$ solve (closed-form $2\times2$ for $P{=}2$, an unrolled
no-pivot LU above that), the NB log-likelihood and $\log\alpha$-gradient
(\texttt{lgamma} and \texttt{log1p} from \texttt{libdevice}, \texttt{digamma}
hand-implemented to match ATen's \texttt{calc\_digamma} through a recurrence to
$x\ge10$ followed by the Bernoulli asymptotic series), and the Armijo line
search. A per-gene \texttt{while} loop lets each gene exit as soon as it
converges, so the kernel does not run every gene to the batch maximum. The kernel
is fp64 and covers $P\in\{2,\dots,6\}$; \texttt{fit\_dispersions} gates on the
covered range and routes wider designs to the eager path, while the kernel's own
launcher raises instead of fitting a truncated model, since it reads a fixed
number of design columns. At the gene-wise step, non-converged and
high-dispersion genes get the same noIncrease revert and grid fallback as the
eager path, computed eagerly after the kernel returns; at the MAP step neither
applies, in either mode, because R applies neither. The fused reduction order and
the hand-written \texttt{digamma} move the last bits, so this mode is not
bit-identical to eager, and Sec.~\ref{sec:parity} reports how far the two
diverge on real data.

\subsection{GLM acceleration}
\label{sec:glm}
The GLM is implemented as a batched negative-binomial IRLS solve on the GPU,
using one batched factorization across genes at each iteration. Genes that
diverge or reach the DESeq2 iteration cap use the same host-side fallback as the
reference; only those exceptional gene rows are transferred. Cook's robust
dispersion, distance, count replacement, and the affected-gene refit remain on
the GPU in CUDA workflows. Wald standard errors retain the $\mu\ge0.5$ floor in
\texttt{fitBeta.cpp}; LRTs use the full--reduced deviance difference.

\subsection{LFC shrinkage acceleration}
\label{sec:apeglm}
apeGLM \citep{zhu2019apeglm} is a per-gene optimization, so its L-BFGS state,
line search, and convergence flags are batched on the GPU. We match the
reference optimizer's initialization and stopping rules because extreme,
low-count genes can have competing posterior modes. As in \texttt{lfcShrink},
only the shrunken coefficient and its standard error are replaced; p-values
remain those from the Wald fit. Loss and gradient share the same large
gene--sample linear predictor, invariant count/dispersion terms are hoisted out
of the optimizer loop, and fitted size factors and Wald inference are reused
rather than reconstructed downstream. On CUDA, PyTorch compilation fuses the
elementwise likelihood, score, and final-Hessian work surrounding the matrix
products. This removes repeated gene--sample temporaries while retaining the
same L-BFGS initialization, line search, stopping rules, and fp64 outputs; CPU
execution keeps the eager implementation.

\subsection{Validation and benchmark design}
\label{sec:parity-method}
Reference fixtures were generated with R~DESeq2~1.52.0: three single-factor
designs at $12\times200$,
$30\times500$ and $60\times2000$ (samples $\times$ genes, $P=2$), a
$60\times1500$ two-factor design $\sim$\,batch\,$+$\,condition with a
three-level batch ($P=4$), and a $60\times1000$ continuous-covariate design
($P=2$), using the seeds 0, 0, 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Every intermediate
is checked against R: size factors,
\texttt{dispGeneEst}, \texttt{dispFit} (the parametric trend on every fixture,
plus the \texttt{"local"} and \texttt{"mean"} trends on the single-factor
ones), \texttt{dispMAP}, the final dispersion and its outlier set, $\beta$,
$\mu$, the hat-matrix diagonals, Cook's distances, Wald SE, the LRT statistic on
all five designs, the independent-filtering \texttt{padj}, and the apeGLM prior
scale and shrunk LFC/SE. Errors are reported as p95 relative values, or absolute
values where a quantity crosses zero. Sec.~\ref{sec:parity} reports the measured
agreement for the principal quantities and tabulates the five pipeline stages on
the real datasets (Table~\ref{tab:parity}). For real-data summaries, max
relative error is used for positive size factors; p95 relative error for
positive dispersions; p95 absolute error for LFCs, which cross zero; Jaccard
similarity of finite $\mathrm{padj}<0.05$ sets; and Pearson correlation over
finite pairs for shrunken LFCs. Empty called-set unions are assigned Jaccard
similarity one. Eager, graph, and Triton modes were evaluated separately
against the same reference and acceptance thresholds.

Performance was evaluated on one NVIDIA A100-SXM4-40GB under PyTorch~2.7.1,
CUDA~12.6, and NVIDIA driver~580.126.20. The CPU reference was
R~DESeq2~1.52.0 with apeglm~1.34.0 on a 12-vCPU virtual machine. The primary
comparison used the ordinary \texttt{DESeq()}, \texttt{results()}, and
\texttt{lfcShrink()} call sequence with \texttt{MulticoreParam(12)}.
GPU times are medians of five complete, CUDA-synchronized measurements after
one warm-up. The two long-running large-GTEx CPU endpoints are single cold
observations and are interpreted as endpoint comparisons rather than sampling
distributions. Memory-boundary experiments are single fresh-process
feasibility tests.

The study includes six designs derived from three published datasets:
pasilla \citep{brooks2011pasilla} ($n{=}7$; one- and two-factor designs),
airway \citep{himes2014airway} ($n{=}8$; three design slices), and a
300-sample GTEx \citep{gtex2020atlas} whole-blood versus skeletal-muscle
contrast with 54{,}922 genes. GTEx counts were obtained from recount2's
uniform reprocessing of SRA study SRP012682
\citep{colladotorres2017recount2}; the primary contrast contains 150 samples
per tissue selected using R seed 1. Base coverage was converted to read counts
using each sample's average read length, with 100 bases used for missing or
non-positive values. A fixed-seed simulation independently varies sample count
at 2{,}000 genes. Finally, nested cohorts from all 9{,}662 GTEx source samples
and 54 tissue levels vary sample count and design width while holding the gene
axis fixed.

\section{Results}

\subsection{GPU execution preserves DESeq2 results}
\label{sec:parity}
We compared each stage of the pipeline with R~DESeq2~1.52.0 across six
real-data designs derived from three datasets (Table~\ref{tab:parity}).
Agreement was evaluated separately for normalization, dispersion estimation,
GLM fitting, significance calls, and LFC shrinkage.

\label{tab:parity}
\begin{tabular}{llrr}
\toprule
Substep & Metric & Median & Worst \\\midrule

Every eager-mode pipeline stage met its predefined tolerance across all six
real-data designs.
Normalization was effectively identical to R, and the largest dispersion
discrepancy was \fact{0.44\%} at the 95th percentile. Raw LFC estimates differed
by at most \fact{$8.1\times10^{-5}$} at the 95th percentile, and
significant-gene sets had Jaccard similarity of at least \fact{0.99963}.
Shrunken LFCs had Pearson correlation of at least \fact{0.99722} with R.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The principal finding is that the full supported DESeq2 workflow can exploit
GPU parallelism without substituting a different statistical test. Across six
real-data designs, \cudeseq{} preserved normalization, dispersion estimation,
GLM coefficients, significance calls, and apeGLM-shrunken effects while
reducing observed end-to-end wall time by \fact{3.6--35.1$\times$} relative to
12-worker R~DESeq2. Agreement at intermediate stages is important here: similar
final gene lists alone could conceal compensating differences in dispersion,
standard-error, or multiple-testing calculations.

The results identify the gene axis as the useful unit of parallelism for this
class of count model. Basic batching exposes thousands of independent fits to
the GPU, but dispersion estimation also illustrates why batching alone is
insufficient. Its small matrix operations and variable-length optimization
loops can be limited by dispatch overhead rather than arithmetic throughput.
CUDA-graph replay reduces that overhead without changing eager arithmetic,
whereas the Triton implementation fuses the complete per-gene loop and permits
independent convergence. The relative benefit varies with design width and
sample count because the balance shifts among dispersion, GLM fitting,
significance processing, and LFC shrinkage. This explains why stage-level
acceleration does not translate into a constant end-to-end speedup and points
to shrinkage as an important target for further optimization in some designs.

A broader methodological observation is that reference compatibility depends
on both the statistical specification and details of its numerical realization.
The dispersion prior, Wald standard errors, and apeGLM optimization each expose
choices that are not fully determined by the model equations. For example, the
minimum fitted-mean convention used in the Wald weight matrix affects genes
with near-zero fitted counts and can change their standard errors. Conversely,
the Triton kernel evaluates the same dispersion objective as eager execution
but changes floating-point reduction order and the implementation of the
digamma function. Its maximum absolute eager--Triton dispersion difference is
\fact{0.2604} on GTEx and no more than \fact{$3.8 \times 10^{-7}$} across the
five smaller designs. The downstream agreement reported here indicates that
these numerical differences do not constitute a practically meaningful change
to inference on the evaluated data, but it does not imply bitwise identity.

The GTEx experiments extend the conclusion beyond the small public examples.
At 54{,}922 genes, the fused path supported a 2{,}451-sample, six-tissue model
in less than half the memory of the 40-GB A100, while wider eager models reached
the memory boundary between $P=9$ and $P=10$ in the nested cohorts. This result
separates computational feasibility from statistical scope: adding samples can
improve the information available to a model, but increasing design width also
raises the cost of every per-gene solve and the size of resident intermediates.
Chunking, mixed precision where statistically acceptable, or a wider fused
kernel may move this boundary, but each would require renewed parity analysis.

The practical value is greatest when the same reference analysis is applied
repeatedly, including perturbation screens, resampling studies, and evaluation
of generated expression profiles. \cudeseq{} is therefore complementary to
methods that change the inferential model for scalability: it provides a path
for retaining DESeq2 semantics when compatibility with established analyses is
the priority. For isolated small experiments, GPU availability, transfer
costs, and software complexity may outweigh the wall-time benefit.

Several limitations bound the present conclusions. Measurements use one A100
and one 12-vCPU host, so absolute time, speedup, energy use, and cost cannot be
generalized to other hardware. Five of the six primary designs contain only
seven or eight samples, and the larger scaling study derives from a single
GTEx source rather than independent cohorts. Interactions, observation weights,
the \texttt{ashr} and normal shrinkage estimators, and the \texttt{glmGamPoi}
dispersion backend remain outside the validated scope. The fused Triton path
supports $P\in\{2,\dots,6\}$; wider designs use eager dispersion. Finally, the
large-GTEx CPU endpoints and memory-boundary points are single observations,
and the tissue series changes sample count and design width together.

In conclusion, gene-batched GPU execution can substantially accelerate
reference-compatible negative-binomial differential expression. The findings
support the use of DESeq2-style inference in high-throughput settings where
computational cost might otherwise motivate a less comparable surrogate.

2.20\,GHz, 6 physical cores and 12 logical CPUs, 83\,GiB RAM, and Linux~5.10.

timings are medians of five measured repetitions following an untimed warm-up.

are explicitly single-run measurements. The separate synthetic sample-axis sweep uses seven measured
repetitions after three warm-ups and records its software stack in its artifact.
