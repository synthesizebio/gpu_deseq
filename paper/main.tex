\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\textbf{Motivation:} DESeq2 is widely used for bulk RNA-seq
differential-expression analysis. Its dispersion and negative-binomial GLM
fits are performed gene by gene and can be slow when many analyses are needed.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Results:} \cudeseq{} implements the standard DESeq2 Wald workflow in
PyTorch and batches the gene-wise calculations on a CUDA GPU. Against
R~DESeq2~1.52.0, size factors agreed to floating-point precision and median
relative dispersion error was \fact{8.5e-4} across six real designs (7--300
samples; $P=2$--$5$). All \fact{76/76 step-parity tests} on synthetic fixtures
passed. The workflow includes count-outlier replacement and refitting. On an
A100, end-to-end runtime was \fact{10.3--99.2$\times$} lower than
single-threaded R.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Availability and implementation:} \texttt{gpu-deseq} requires PyTorch and
a CUDA GPU, and is open source under the MIT license at
\url{https://github.com/synthesizebio/gpu_deseq}, with reproduction scripts in
\texttt{bench/} and \texttt{benchmarks/}.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Contact:} max@synthesize.bio\\[3pt]
\textbf{Supplementary information:} Appended to this manuscript.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
DESeq2 \citep{love2014deseq2} fits a negative-binomial model to bulk RNA-seq
counts. A Wald analysis estimates size factors and dispersions, fits the model,
tests coefficients, and can shrink log fold changes with apeGLM
\citep{zhu2019apeglm}. Dispersion fitting, GLM fitting, and shrinkage are
iterative calculations that can be parallelized across genes. The R package
runs them on the CPU.

This cost matters when differential expression is evaluated repeatedly, for
example in benchmarks of gene-expression and virtual-cell models
\citep{bunne2024virtualcell,roohani2025vcc}. Some such benchmarks use rank
tests on normalized expression instead \citep{roohani2025vcc,wei2026perturbench}.



\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/gpt_schematic.png}
\caption{\cudeseq{} implements the five stages of a DESeq2 analysis. The
gene-wise dispersion, GLM, and apeGLM calculations are batched on the GPU.
Dispersion fitting can use eager PyTorch, CUDA-graph replay, or a fused Triton
kernel (Table~\ref{tab:modes}).}
\label{fig:schematic}
\end{figure}

glmGamPoi \citep{ahlmanneltze2020glmgampoi} provides a faster CPU
Gamma--Poisson fit and can be used by DESeq2. PyDESeq2
\citep{muzellec2023pydeseq2} reimplements DESeq2 in Python, with results that
are similar but not identical to the R package. GPU single-cell packages such
as ScaleSC accelerate PCA, graph construction, and clustering
\citep{hu2025scalesc}; they do not target bulk negative-binomial inference.

\cudeseq{} is a PyTorch implementation of this inference path. It follows
R~DESeq2~1.52.0 at intermediate steps and offers eager, CUDA-graph
\citep{nvidia2024cudagraphs}, and Triton \citep{tillet2019triton} dispersion
paths. The largest real-data p95 relative dispersion error was
$4.4\times10^{-3}$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:parity}).

\section{Methods}

\subsection{The DESeq2 pipeline and its cost profile}
\label{sec:pipeline}
DESeq2 \citep{love2014deseq2} models each gene independently with a
negative-binomial GLM. For gene $g$ with counts $K_{gi}$ over samples
$i=1,\dots,S$, design matrix $X\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times P}$, and size factors
$s_i$, it assumes $K_{gi}\sim\mathrm{NB}(\mu_{gi},\alpha_g)$ with
$\mu_{gi}=s_i\exp(x_i^{\!\top}\beta_g)$ and variance
$\mu_{gi}+\alpha_g\mu_{gi}^2$, and runs five stages. We write $S$ for the sample
count and $P$ for the number of model terms throughout; where a formula mirrors
R's own source we keep R's names $m\equiv S$ and $p\equiv P$ for it, and dataset
sizes are quoted as $n\equiv S$.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{Normalization}: median-of-ratios size factors.
  \item \textbf{Dispersion estimation}: gene-wise Cox--Reid MLE, a parametric
        mean--dispersion trend, empirical-Bayes MAP shrinkage, and an outlier
        rule. This is the dominant cost on the largest design tested here.
  \item \textbf{GLM fit / testing}: IRLS negative-binomial GLM, Wald / LRT.
  \item \textbf{Significance}: Cook's-distance filter, independent filtering,
        Benjamini--Hochberg adjustment.
  \item \textbf{LFC shrinkage}: apeGLM \citep{zhu2019apeglm}, a Cauchy-prior
        MAP estimate of the effect size.
\end{enumerate}
Stages 2, 3 and 5 contain per-gene iterative optimizations, and each is
parallel across genes. On the largest design, dispersion estimation dominates
the wall time (Sec.~\ref{sec:realtiming}) because its gene-wise MLE maximizes the Cox--Reid
adjusted profile log-likelihood
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\mathrm{CR}}(\alpha_g)=\sum_{i=1}^{S}\log \mathrm{NB}\!\bigl(K_{gi};\mu_{gi},\alpha_g\bigr)
\;-\;\tfrac12\log\det\!\bigl(X^{\!\top} W_g X\bigr),
\label{eq:cr}
\end{equation}
with $W_g=\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\mu_{gi}/(1+\alpha_g\mu_{gi})\bigr)$. The
$\log\det$ Cox--Reid adjustment couples every sample and has to be recomputed at
each iteration.

\subsection{GPU implementation}
\label{sec:port}
We reproduce the reference computation step by step so that intermediate
quantities agree with R (Sec.~\ref{sec:parity-method}). In the eager tensor
path, each per-gene loop in R becomes a program over all $G$ genes at once: the per-gene
solver state ($\log\alpha_g$, $\beta_g$, step sizes, iteration counters,
convergence flags) is carried as $(G,\cdot)$ tensors, and a gene that has
converged is frozen by a mask so that its later updates are no-ops.  Batch speeds are constrained
to the slowest gene's iteration count. Genes that are zero in
every sample are dropped before fitting and their outputs padded with NaN, as in
R.

Three of the five stages need no adjustment beyond this batching.
\emph{Normalization} computes median-of-ratios size factors from a $0.5$ quantile
rather than a median primitive, so that the averaging of the two middle values at
even sample counts matches R; when every gene carries at least one zero it falls
back to the positive-counts geometric mean. The \emph{GLM fit} is a batched IRLS
negative-binomial solve: working weights $W=\mu/(1+\alpha\mu)$, working response
$z=\log(\mu/s)+(K-\mu)/\mu$, and normal equations
$(X^{\!\top}WX+\lambda I)\beta=X^{\!\top}Wz$ solved for all genes in one batched
factorization (ridge $\lambda=10^{-6}$, deviance-ratio convergence
$<\!10^{-8}$, at most $250$ iterations; a gene passing $|\beta|=30$ is marked
diverged). It returns $\beta$, an unthresholded $\mu$, which Cook's distances need
raw, and the hat-matrix diagonals; the few genes that reach the cap or diverge are
re-fit one at a time by L-BFGS-B on the host, mirroring DESeq2's
\texttt{optim}-based fallback for the same genes. Wald standard errors follow from
the sandwich form
$\mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{c^{\!\top}(M{+}\lambda I)^{-1}M(M{+}\lambda I)^{-1}c}$, with
$M=X^{\!\top}\tilde WX$ built from a $\mu$ floored at $0.5$ to match
\texttt{fitBeta.cpp}. Without that floor a sample with $\mu<0.5$ contributes
almost no weight instead of a small one, $M$ effectively loses it, and the SE
inflates on degenerate genes (Sec.~\ref{sec:deplots}). The LRT statistic is the
full/reduced deviance difference.

\emph{Significance} contains no per-gene optimizer; Cook's distances and the BH
adjustment are closed-form. A gene is flagged when its largest Cook's distance
over samples in design cells of at least three replicates exceeds
$F_{0.99,P,S-P}$, computed against a trimmed method-of-moments dispersion floored
at $0.04$, and fewer than three samples carry more counts than the sample holding
that maximum. Independent filtering sweeps 50 base-mean quantile cutoffs, runs BH
at each, and selects the first whose rejection count comes within one RMS residual
of the peak of a robust LOWESS fit to the rejection curve.

The public \texttt{deseq()} entry point runs the standard Wald path:
normalization, dispersion estimation, the Wald fit, Cook's-distance replacement
and refitting for eligible design cells, and result assembly. The lower-level
functions remain available for stage-wise validation and for LRT analyses. We
use ``drop-in'' in this computational sense: the standard input model and
reported DE quantities are reproduced. It does not imply that the Python API
accepts every R object or every optional DESeq2 estimator
(Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Batched dispersion estimation}
\label{sec:disp}
The dispersion stage is a batched port of DESeq2's \texttt{fitDisp} and the
\texttt{estimateDispersions} logic around it. Two inputs must be built the way R
builds them, because the fit's accept-and-revert behavior depends on where the
optimizer starts: the initial dispersion is
$\mathrm{clip}\bigl(\min(\alpha_{\text{rough}},\alpha_{\text{MoM}}),10^{-8},10\bigr)$
from a linear-regression and a method-of-moments estimate, and $\hat\mu$ comes from
plain linear regression for saturated designs and one IRLS pass at the initial
dispersion otherwise. The stage has three parts.

\textbf{(i) Gene-wise Cox--Reid MLE.} We maximize Eq.~\ref{eq:cr} over
$a=\log\alpha$ by gradient ascent with Armijo backtracking. Each iteration proposes
$a'=a+\kappa\,\partial_a\ell_{\mathrm{CR}}$, shrinking $\kappa$ as needed to keep
$a'$ inside $[-30,10]$, accepts when the Armijo condition holds
($\varepsilon=10^{-4}$), and adapts the step: on acceptance
$\kappa\leftarrow\min(1.1\kappa,\kappa_0)$ with $\kappa_0=1$ and a halving every
fifth acceptance, on rejection $\kappa\leftarrow\kappa/2$. A gene stops once an
accepted step improves the objective by less than $10^{-6}$ or $a$ falls below
$\log(\mathrm{minDisp}/10)$, capped at $100$ iterations. Two R-matching safeguards
follow: a \emph{noIncrease revert} restores the initial estimate for genes whose
objective never improved on it, and a \emph{grid fallback} re-fits the genes R
would flag as non-converged (those at the cap or with a single accepted step), whose
dispersion still exceeds $10\cdot\mathrm{minDisp}$, using a deterministic
$100$-point coarse grid over $\log\alpha$ refined by a second. Estimates are
clipped to $[10^{-8},\max(10,S)]$: R's ceiling is $\max(10,S)$, and a fixed $10$
truncates legitimate dispersions above ten samples.

\textbf{(ii) Parametric trend.} We fit $\alpha\approx a_0+a_1/\bar\mu$ over the
genes with $\mathrm{dispGeneEst}>100\cdot\mathrm{minDisp}$ from
$(a_0,a_1)=(0.1,1)$. Each iteration forms residuals $\alpha/(a_0+a_1/\bar\mu)$,
keeps the genes whose residual lies in $(10^{-4},15)$, and re-fits a Gamma-family,
identity-link GLM warm-started at the current coefficients, stopping when
$\sum\log(\text{coef}/\text{coef}_{\text{old}})^2<10^{-6}$ with the inner GLM
converged, or after ten iterations; a trimmed-mean trend is the fallback. R's other
two \texttt{fitType} settings are also implemented: \texttt{"local"}, a LOWESS of
$\log\alpha$ on $\log\bar\mu$ at span $0.3$ with three robustness iterations, and
\texttt{"mean"}, which forces the trimmed-mean trend.

\textbf{(iii) MAP shrinkage.} A log-normal prior centered on the trend shrinks
the gene-wise estimates. Its variance follows R's three regimes, each built from
the MAD $s$ of the log-residuals: the closed form
$\max\!\bigl(s^2-\psi_1(\tfrac{m-p}{2}),\,0.25\bigr)$ at ample residual degrees
of freedom, a stochastic KL-divergence grid search when $0<m-p\le3$, and $s^2$
itself for a saturated design. The MAP step re-runs the same gradient ascent from
$\log\mathrm{dispGeneEst}$ with the prior term enabled and, matching R, with the
revert and the grid fallback disabled. An outlier rule then keeps the raw MLE
wherever $\log\alpha_{\text{MLE}}$ exceeds the trend by more than $2s$.

The KL-divergence branch is the only stochastic part of the pipeline. It is used
when $0<m-p\le3$ to estimate the dispersion-prior variance. We reproduce R's
random-number stream and its \texttt{loess} interpolation because substitutions
changed the estimate. On \texttt{airway} ($m{=}8$, $p{=}5$), the implementation
returns the same prior variance as R. The random-stream replay is sequential and
adds about 0.3~s per dataset. Implementation details and the validation of this
branch are provided in the supplementary algorithms and tests.

\subsection{Three execution modes for the dispersion loop}
\label{sec:modes}
The gradient-ascent loop is where the launch-bound overhead concentrates
(Sec.~\ref{sec:realtiming}), so it is the only stage with alternative execution
backends. The three modes
compute the same mathematics and differ in how the loop is dispatched
(Table~\ref{tab:modes}). They are selected per call
(\texttt{fit\_dispersions(..., use\_cuda\_graph, use\_triton)}) or through the
\texttt{GPU\_DESEQ\_ACCEL} environment variable.

\subsection{CUDA-graph chunked replay}
\label{sec:graph}
The graph backend captures ten optimizer iterations and replays the captured
chunk until convergence. This avoids a full 100-iteration capture while reducing
launch overhead. Genes that converge within a chunk remain in the graph as no-op
updates until the next convergence check.

CUDA graphs cannot capture the required cuSOLVER calls. We therefore use a
no-pivot LU routine for the Cox--Reid determinant and trace terms in both eager
and graph modes. The graph cache is keyed by matrix shape, chunk size, prior
state, dtype, and device. The graph and eager paths are bit-identical.

\subsection{Triton full-loop fusion}
\label{sec:triton}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/triton_dispersion_schematic.png}
\caption{Fused Triton dispersion fitting. One program is launched per gene and
runs the Cox--Reid optimizer in registers. The trend fit remains outside the
kernel. Supplementary Algorithms~\ref{alg:triton-overview}--\ref{alg:triton-detail}
give pseudocode.}
\label{fig:triton}
\end{figure}

The Triton backend launches one fp64 program per gene (Fig.~\ref{fig:triton}).
The program evaluates the likelihood, Cox--Reid term, gradient, and Armijo line
search in registers, and exits when that gene converges. It supports
$P\in\{2,\dots,6\}$; wider designs use the eager backend. The same fallback and
revert rules are applied after the gene-wise fit.

The fused reduction order and the Triton \texttt{digamma} implementation change
the final bits, so Triton is not bit-identical to eager. At fixed $\alpha$, its
log-posterior and gradient agree with eager to \fact{3e-15} and \fact{3e-14}
relative, respectively. The real-data comparison is reported in
Sec.~\ref{sec:parity}.

\subsection{Batched port of the apeGLM optimizer}
\label{sec:apeglm}
apeGLM \citep{zhu2019apeglm} shrinks the log-fold-change by maximizing a per-gene
posterior: the NB likelihood times a heavy-tailed Cauchy prior on the coefficient
of interest and a wide Gaussian (SD $15$, the DESeq2 default) on the nuisance
coefficients. The Cauchy scale is set by empirical Bayes,
$\tau=\min(\sqrt{\widehat{\text{var}}},1)$, with $\widehat{\text{var}}$ the root
of apeGLM's prior-variance equation in the MLE coefficients and their Wald
standard errors, solved once per dataset on the host.

For extreme-effect, low-count genes, the apeGLM posterior can have two nearly
equal modes. R reports the solution reached by its initialized L-BFGS optimizer,
not necessarily the global optimum. \cudeseq{} therefore uses the same optimizer
family, initialization, stopping criteria, and iteration limit, vectorized across
genes on the GPU. This matches the reference shrinkage behavior more closely than
a different optimizer.

As in \texttt{lfcShrink}, only the shrunken coefficient and its standard error
are replaced; p-values remain those from the Wald fit.

\subsection{Parity methodology}
\label{sec:parity-method}
Reference fixtures are generated by \texttt{scripts/generate\_r\_fixtures.R}
against R~DESeq2~1.52.0: three single-factor designs at $12\times200$,
$30\times500$ and $60\times2000$ (samples $\times$ genes, $P=2$), a
$60\times1500$ two-factor design $\sim$\,batch\,$+$\,condition with a
three-level batch ($P=4$), and a $60\times1000$ continuous-covariate design
($P=2$). Every intermediate is checked against R: size factors,
\texttt{dispGeneEst}, \texttt{dispFit} (the parametric trend on every fixture,
plus the \texttt{"local"} and \texttt{"mean"} trends on the single-factor
ones), \texttt{dispMAP}, the final dispersion and its outlier set, $\beta$,
$\mu$, the hat-matrix diagonals, Cook's distances, Wald SE, the LRT statistic on
all five designs, the independent-filtering \texttt{padj}, and the apeGLM prior
scale and shrunk LFC/SE. Errors are reported as p95 relative values, or absolute
values where a quantity crosses zero. Sec.~\ref{sec:parity} reports the measured
agreement for the principal quantities and tabulates the five pipeline stages on
the real datasets (Table~\ref{tab:parity}).

\section{Results}

\subsection{Experimental setup}
All GPU measurements are taken on one NVIDIA A100-SXM4-40GB under PyTorch~2.6
with CUDA~12.4 and NVIDIA driver~550.90.07. The reference CPU baseline
(host~A) is R~DESeq2~1.52.0 on a
12-core box, with R~4.6.0 and apeglm~1.34.0. Every timing is a warm median of $N$
repetitions with \texttt{torch.cuda.synchronize()} on both sides of the timed
region, so no GPU work is left in flight when the clock stops. R runs
single-threaded in the primary comparisons.

We validate on three real, published RNA-seq datasets sliced into six designs
spanning $P=2$--$5$ model terms: pasilla \citep{brooks2011pasilla}
(\emph{Drosophila}, $n{=}7$, one- and two-factor), airway
\citep{himes2014airway} (human, $n{=}8$, three design slices), and a 300-sample
GTEx \citep{gtex2020atlas} whole-blood vs.\ skeletal-muscle contrast
($54{,}922$ genes). The first two are distributed as Bioconductor experiment
packages; the GTEx counts are recount2's uniform reprocessing of SRA study
SRP012682 \citep{colladotorres2017recount2}, from which we draw 150 samples per
tissue at a fixed seed and convert base coverage to read counts by dividing by
the average read length. Scaling studies use fixed-seed negative-binomial
simulations where the gene and sample axes can be swept independently.

\subsection{Numerical parity with R}
\label{sec:parity}
\begin{sloppypar}
Correctness is checked per substep against R on all six real datasets
(Table~\ref{tab:parity}). A separate synthetic
step-parity suite (\texttt{tests/test\_r\_step\_parity.py}, 76/76) confirms the
same agreement at controlled sizes, and more tightly: size factors agree to
$<$1e-10, gene-wise dispersion to $\sim$1e-9, and MAP dispersion, $\beta$, $\mu$,
the Hessian and Wald SEs to $\sim$1e-6 relative. A further suite
(\texttt{tests/test\_triton\_dispersion.py}) pins the fused kernel against the
eager path once per covered $P$, the axis along which its specialized arms
(Sec.~\ref{sec:triton}) could diverge without any other test noticing.
\end{sloppypar}

\subsection{Scaling on synthetic data}
\label{sec:roofline}
Synthetic data separates the gene and sample axes. Hardware counters were not
available, so we used an empirical roofline. A fp64 streaming probe sustained
\fact{1369\,GB/s} (\fact{88\%} of the A100 specification), while the dispersion
kernels used 3--43\% of that bandwidth. The objective-and-gradient evaluation
took \fact{1292\,\textmu s} at 2,000 genes and \fact{1287\,\textmu s} at
20,000 genes. This weak dependence on gene count is consistent with launch and
dispatch overhead dominating the loop.

The synthetic scaling experiments below isolate the dispersion optimizer rather
than report end-to-end DESeq2 speedups; they therefore do not exercise the
standard replacement/refit wrapper branch. The real-data timings in
Table~\ref{tab:realtiming} use that branch where applicable.
If the loop is launch-bound, the accelerators should pay most where iterations are
many and each is cheap, and fade as every iteration acquires enough arithmetic to
fill the device. The sample axis tests that directly
(Table~\ref{tab:samplesweep}). Holding genes at $2{,}000$ and sweeping $S$ from a
$2\mathrm{v}2$ pilot to a $2{,}000$-sample cohort, the graph's speedup on the
isolated gene-wise ascent loop declines from \fact{7.6$\times$} at $S{=}4$ to
\fact{0.96$\times$} at $S{=}2000$: by two thousand samples the launch overhead it
removes is a rounding error against the per-iteration work, and replay is no longer
worth its bookkeeping. Triton decays over the same range but never loses, from
\fact{69.0$\times$} to \fact{7.7$\times$}, since fusion removes the intermediate
traffic as well as the launches. Two features of the sweep are structural rather
than noise. The $S{=}4$ and $S{=}6$ rows run the full \fact{100}-iteration cap
while every larger $S$ converges in \fact{13--25}, which is most of why the
small-sample regime is both the slowest and the most accelerable. And $S{=}4$ is
the one row where the stage-level speedup ($1.8\times$) falls far below the
loop-level one ($69.0\times$): with $m-p=2$ that design takes the stochastic KL
branch (Sec.~\ref{sec:disp}), whose compiled replay of R's random stream is serial
host work no GPU mode touches, contributing $\approx$\fact{470\,ms} to every mode
at $S{=}4$ and nothing at $S{=}6$. It is Amdahl's remainder for that row.

The gene axis says the same thing from the other side. Taking $2{,}000$ to
$20{,}000$ genes at fixed $S{=}60$ multiplies the work tenfold but the eager stage
time only by \fact{1.7$\times$} at $P{=}2$ (\fact{127}$\to$\fact{210}\,ms) and
\fact{1.2$\times$} at $P{=}4$ (\fact{193}$\to$\fact{224}\,ms): nine tenths of the
added genes are absorbed into launch latency the small case already paid. Because
that fixed overhead is amortized over more genes at $20{,}000$, it is also where
the graph has least left to remove, its stage speedup dropping from
\fact{4.4$\times$} to \fact{1.7$\times$} at $P{=}2$ (\fact{3.7$\times$} to
\fact{2.2$\times$} at $P{=}4$). Graph capture is a warm-path optimization: the
one-time capture costs \fact{487--796\,ms} across these four configurations, so it
is recovered only when many matrices of one shape are fitted in a session, as in the
serving pattern the virtual-cell evaluation loops of the Introduction generate.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:ablations}
This section examines chunk length, per-gene early exit, and the apeGLM optimizer.

\textbf{Chunk length.} A full-length capture performed poorly in this benchmark.
Because a captured graph cannot branch on a convergence
flag, a capture of $k$ iterations must be replayed $\lceil \mathrm{iters}/k \rceil$
times, so the loop executes $\lceil \mathrm{iters}/k \rceil \cdot k$ iterations
rather than the $\mathrm{iters}$ eager early-exits after; done genes are frozen
no-ops but still cost their share of the kernel. Sweeping $k$ over the divisors of
$\mathrm{maxit}=100$ (Table~\ref{tab:chunksweep}) traces that cost model. The
full-length capture $k{=}100$ rounds $26$ real iterations up to $100$ and gives
back almost all the benefit: \fact{1.83$\times$} instead of \fact{6.01$\times$} at
$2{,}000$ genes, \fact{0.99$\times$} instead of \fact{2.42$\times$} at $20{,}000$,
where it is indistinguishable from not graphing at all. What the sweep shows is
that the padded iteration count, not the replay count, sets the time: rows
executing the same number of iterations land within a couple of per cent of each
other however many replays that took ($k{=}25$ and $k{=}50$ both pad to $50$ and
differ by $0.4\%$ at $2{,}000$ genes; $k{=}2$ through $k{=}20$ all pad to $40$ and
differ by $2\%$ at $20{,}000$). At $2{,}000$ genes $k{=}2$ is fastest
(\fact{6.01$\times$}) because it avoids padding, whereas at $20{,}000$ genes
$k{=}10$ and $k{=}20$ tie at \fact{2.42$\times$}. The current $k{=}10$ default
is therefore a conservative compromise rather than a universal optimum.

\textbf{Per-gene early exit in the fused kernel.} The Triton kernel's loop is
guarded per program by \texttt{(it < maxit) \& (\textasciitilde done)}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:triton}) rather than run for a fixed trip count, and the iteration
counts of Table~\ref{tab:samplesweep} bound what that is worth: outside the
small-sample regime the loop converges in \fact{13--25} iterations against a cap
of \fact{100}, so a fixed-trip kernel would execute four to nearly eight times
the loop body for an identical answer. The saving is largest exactly where the
batch is widest, since a fixed trip count makes every gene wait for the cap
rather than for its own convergence. This is a bound inferred from the shipped
kernel's measured iteration counts rather than a measured A/B: the released code
exposes no fixed-trip switch to compare against.

\textbf{The apeGLM optimizer is a parity choice, not a speed choice.} A batched
Newton solver for the shrinkage step is both simpler to write and faster than
porting apeGLM's L-BFGS, and it disagrees with R. The
reason is the one described in Sec.~\ref{sec:apeglm}: the Cauchy-prior posterior
is bimodal for extreme-effect genes, R reports whichever mode its own optimizer
reaches from its own initialization, and a solver that finds the global optimum
therefore returns a better estimate that is not the reference's. This is the one
place where improving on the reference and reproducing it are in direct conflict,
and a drop-in replacement has to choose reproduction. Porting the reference's
optimizer restores agreement to $r\ge$\fact{0.997} on shrunk log-fold-changes
across all six designs (Table~\ref{tab:parity}) while keeping the stage on the
GPU with no per-gene host fallback.

\section{Discussion}
\cudeseq{} uses fp64 and follows the R convergence tolerances, clamps, and
optimization steps. Across the six real datasets, size factors, dispersions,
coefficients, Wald statistics, adjusted p-values, and shrunken effects agreed
closely with R (Table~\ref{tab:parity}). The observed end-to-end speedup on the
A100 was \fact{10.3--99.2$\times$} relative to single-threaded R.

At the tested sizes, dispersion fitting is limited largely by kernel-launch and
dispatch overhead. CUDA graphs and Triton reduce that overhead. Their benefit
falls as the per-iteration calculation becomes larger. The reported ratios are
specific to the A100, the CPU baseline, and single-threaded R; they should not
be treated as hardware-independent estimates.

Compatibility depends on implementation details as well as the statistical
model. Examples include R's random-number stream and \texttt{loess} behavior
for the dispersion prior, the $\mu$ floor in \texttt{fitBeta.cpp}, and apeGLM's
optimizer initialization. Omitting these details changed outputs on difficult
genes even when the underlying equations were the same.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item Tested designs: single factor (up to four levels), additive
        multi-factor (up to $P{=}5$), continuous. Not yet: interaction designs.
        Parity is not tested below $n{=}7$, the smallest real design here; the
        synthetic fixtures start at 12 samples.
  \item Not implemented: \texttt{lfcShrink} types
        \texttt{normal}/\texttt{ashr}, \texttt{glmGamPoi}, VST/rlog.
  \item Triton path: $P\in\{2,\dots,6\}$ (falls back to eager otherwise); not
        bit-identical to eager; agreement is $\le$3.2e-7 in dispersion on the
        five small-$n$ sets, with a worst case of 2.6e-1 relative on one
        \texttt{gtex} gene.
  \item Benchmarks: a single GPU (one A100), so we cannot report how the three
        modes reorder on a smaller or newer device. Parity is measured against
        DESeq2~1.52.0.
\end{itemize}

The remaining work includes the \texttt{normal} and \texttt{ashr} shrinkage
estimators, interaction designs, and Triton support beyond $P{=}6$. The
independent-filtering and dispersion-trend calculations remain on the host.
Multi-GPU execution has not been evaluated.
