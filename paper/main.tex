\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\textbf{Motivation:} DESeq2 is widely used for bulk RNA-seq
differential-expression (DE) analysis. Its dispersion and generalized linear
model (GLM) fits operate independently by gene, but the reference
implementation executes this work on the CPU. Repeated DE analyses can
therefore dominate evaluations of gene-expression models.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Results:} We present \cudeseq, a  PyTorch reimplementation
that batches DESeq2's per-gene work on the GPU while reproducing the reference
step by step. Against R~DESeq2~1.52.0 the closed-form stages agree to
to \fact{8.5e-4} median relative error,
over six real designs (7--300 samples, $P=2$--$5$). Stage-specific validation
covered normalization, dispersion fitting, GLM fitting, significance calls, and
LFC shrinkage. The standard Wald pipeline includes
\texttt{DESeq()}'s count-outlier replacement and per-gene refit. On one A100
\cudeseq{} is \fact{3.1--13.8$\times$} faster end-to-end than the faster of
one-worker and 12-worker R across the six designs.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Availability and implementation:} \texttt{gpu-deseq} requires PyTorch and
a CUDA GPU, and is open source under the MIT license at
\url{https://github.com/synthesizebio/gpu_deseq}, with reproduction scripts in
\texttt{bench/} and \texttt{benchmarks/}.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Contact:} max@synthesize.bio\\[3pt]
\textbf{Supplementary information:} Appended to this manuscript.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
DESeq2 \citep{love2014deseq2} is a standard method for bulk RNA-seq
differential-expression analysis. A typical analyis inlcudes 5 steps: normalization,
dispersion estimation, fitting a negative-binomial GLM, significance testing,
and Log fold change shrinkage estimation \citep{zhu2019apeglm}. The
dispersion, GLM and shrinkage calculations are iterative and independent by
gene. DESeq2 performs these calculations on the CPU; its parallel mode
distributes blocks of genes among worker <cite figure 1>.


Historically, wall time is a secondary concern for a single DE analysis because 
the pipeline is only performed a handful of times per project. However, 
wall time becomes more important when DE must be repeated 1000s ot times
as part of an evaluation loop or comparison of model performance.    
The bioinformatics community has recently seen a surge of interest in 
virtual cells, foundation models for gene expressiond data. 
DE is a natural tool for evaluation of such models, 
and the need to repeat DE analyses in a loop has become more common.
Some recent benchmarks have limited wall time by
utilizing rank-based tests on normalized expression as less expensive
surrogates \citep{roohani2025vcc,wei2026perturbench}. A faster implementation
of the DESeq2 model would permit rigoruous and comprehensive
reference analysis to remain in that an evaluation loop.



\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/gpt_schematic.png}
\caption{Overview of \cudeseq. \emph{Top}, the five stages of a DESeq2 run.
\emph{Bottom}, where the GPU work goes: DESeq2's per-gene, sequential CPU work
(dispersion estimation, the negative-binomial GLM, and apeGLM LFC shrinkage) is
batched across all genes (\emph{left}, solid arrows), and the dispersion fit,

implementation (\emph{right}, dashed arrow). A third mode uses CUDA-graph
chunked replay; the three modes are compared in Table~\ref{tab:modes}. Every stage is
checked against R~DESeq2 step by step (Sec.~\ref{sec:parity}).}
\label{fig:schematic}
\end{figure}

The established route to a faster DESeq2 thus far has been
algorithmic and CPU-bound. glmGamPoi \citep{ahlmanneltze2020glmgampoi} fits the Gamma--Poisson GLM
$6$--$13\times$ faster than DESeq2 and edgeR, and DESeq2 can delegate its
dispersion and GLM fits to it. PyDESeq2 \citep{muzellec2023pydeseq2}, a Python
reimplementation, improves interoperability and speed on large datasets, though by
its authors' own account it yields results ``similar, but not identical'' to
DESeq2; GPU acceleration has meanwhile transformed
adjacent single-cell workflows, where rapids-singlecell and ScaleSC report
$15$--$20\times$ end-to-end speedups \citep{hu2025scalesc}, but those gains come
from the dense and sparse linear-algebra core: PCA, neighbor graphs, clustering.

In this paper we present the following contributions
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,label=(\roman*)]
  \item A batched GPU port of DESeq2's \texttt{fitDisp} (Cox--Reid gradient
        ascent), operation-for-operation faithful to the reference and validated
        against R at every intermediate quantity.
  \item A CUDA-graph mode (chunked replay) that removes launch overhead from the
        dispersion loops, using a capture-safe no-pivot LU in place of cuSOLVER.
  \item A fully fused Triton kernel: one program per gene runs the whole
        gradient-ascent loop in registers with per-gene early exit.
  \item A batched, on-device port of apeGLM's own L-BFGS optimizer (limited-memory
        BFGS with a strong-Wolfe line search).
  \item A per-step benchmark against R and the three GPU
        execution modes, with an empirical roofline analysis.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Reference workflow and supported scope}
\label{sec:pipeline}
DESeq2 \citep{love2014deseq2} models each gene independently with a
negative-binomial GLM. For gene $g$ with counts $K_{gi}$ over samples
$i=1,\dots,S$, design matrix $X\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times P}$, and size factors
$s_i$, it assumes $K_{gi}\sim\mathrm{NB}(\mu_{gi},\alpha_g)$ with
$\mu_{gi}=s_i\exp(x_i^{\!\top}\beta_g)$ and variance
$\mu_{gi}+\alpha_g\mu_{gi}^2$.  The pipeline is composed of five stages. 
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{Normalization}: median-of-ratios size factors.
  \item \textbf{Dispersion estimation}: gene-wise Cox--Reid MLE, a parametric
        mean--dispersion trend, empirical-Bayes MAP shrinkage, and an outlier
        rule.
  \item \textbf{GLM fit / testing}: IRLS negative-binomial GLM, Wald / LRT.
  \item \textbf{Significance}: Cook's-distance filter, independent filtering,
        Benjamini--Hochberg adjustment.
  \item \textbf{LFC shrinkage}: apeGLM \citep{zhu2019apeglm}, a Cauchy-prior
        MAP estimate of the effect size.
\end{enumerate}
This implementation supports the standard DESeq2 Wald workflow, including
Cook's-distance count replacement and refitting where applicable. It also
provides lower-level functions for individual stages and LRT analyses. It does
not aim to accept every R object or optional DESeq2 estimator
(Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}).

Stages 2, 3, and 5 contain independent per-gene iterative calculations. These
are batched on the GPU.

\subsubsection{Normalization}
\label{sec:normalization}
Median-of-ratios normalization is implemented with GPU tensor reductions and
quantiles, including the positive-counts fallback used by DESeq2.

\subsubsection{Dispersion estimation}
\label{sec:disp-overview}
Gene-wise dispersion fitting is batched across genes. The optimization methods
used for this stage are described in Sec.~\ref{sec:disp}.

\subsubsection{GLM fitting and hypothesis testing}
\label{sec:glm-overview}
Negative-binomial IRLS fits and Wald or likelihood-ratio tests are batched over
genes; the implementation details are given in Sec.~\ref{sec:glm}.

\subsubsection{Significance filtering and result assembly}
\label{sec:significance}
Cook's filtering, independent filtering, and multiple-testing adjustment are
implemented as vectorized array operations. The standard wrapper also performs
count replacement and refitting when DESeq2's eligibility conditions are met.

\subsubsection{LFC shrinkage}
\label{sec:apeglm-overview}
The apeGLM optimizer is batched across genes on the GPU; Sec.~\ref{sec:apeglm}
describes the implementation.

\subsection{GPU execution model}
\label{sec:gpu-model}
Counts, normalized counts, and per-gene state remain on the GPU. Independent
gene fits are represented as batched tensors; a mask freezes genes that have
converged while other genes continue. Genes with zero counts in every sample are
excluded from fitting and restored as missing values in the output. This is the
common execution model for dispersion estimation, GLM fitting, and LFC shrinkage.

\subsection{Dispersion acceleration}
\label{sec:disp}
The dispersion stage is a batched port of DESeq2's \texttt{fitDisp} and the
\texttt{estimateDispersions} logic around it. Its gene-wise MLE maximizes the
Cox--Reid adjusted profile log-likelihood
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\mathrm{CR}}(\alpha_g)=\sum_{i=1}^{S}\log \mathrm{NB}\!\bigl(K_{gi};\mu_{gi},\alpha_g\bigr)
\;-\;\tfrac12\log\det\!\bigl(X^{\!\top} W_g X\bigr),
\label{eq:cr}
\end{equation}
where $W_g=\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\mu_{gi}/(1+\alpha_g\mu_{gi})\bigr)$.
We retain DESeq2's initialization, line search, trend fit, MAP shrinkage, and
outlier rule. The optimization target is not the statistical procedure; it is
the repeated gene-wise Cox--Reid evaluation in Eq.~\ref{eq:cr}. The eager,
CUDA-graph, and Triton implementations below use the same optimizer decisions
and differ only in how those evaluations are scheduled.

\paragraph{Execution modes.}
\label{sec:modes}
The gradient-ascent loop is where the launch-bound overhead concentrates
(Sec.~\ref{sec:realtiming}), so it is the only stage with alternative execution
backends. The three modes
compute the same mathematics and differ in how the loop is dispatched
(Table~\ref{tab:modes}). They are selected per call
(\texttt{fit\_dispersions(..., use\_cuda\_graph, use\_triton)}) or through the
\texttt{GPU\_DESEQ\_ACCEL} environment variable.

\paragraph{CUDA-graph chunked replay.}
\label{sec:graph}
In eager mode each gradient-ascent iteration launches a handful of tiny kernels
over $1$--$10$\,MB arrays, so the loop is dominated by launch latency
(Sec.~\ref{sec:roofline}). Capturing the loop as a CUDA graph amortizes that cost
into a single replay, but two obstacles must be overcome.

The first is early exit. A fixed full-length capture would force every gene to
the maximum iteration count, which wastes the typical $15$--$40$-iteration case.
To prevent this we capture a $K{=}10$-iteration graph that reads and writes a set of
persistent state buffers in place, and replay it in a loop with a convergence
check between chunks. A chunk always runs to its boundary, so a gene that
converges partway through executes further iterations.

The second is cuSOLVER. The Cox--Reid term needs $\log\det(b)$ and
$\operatorname{tr}(b^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}b)$ with $b=X^{\!\top}WX$, but
\texttt{slogdet}/\texttt{solve} host-synchronize and cannot be captured. Since $b$
is symmetric positive-definite for full-rank $X$, we replace them with an unrolled
no-pivot LU (\texttt{\_logdet\_and\_tr\_binv\_db}) returning both quantities from
elementwise and indexing operations alone, matching the \texttt{linalg} path to
$\sim$1e-15 for $P=2\dots6$. The eager path uses the same routine, which is what
makes the two bit-identical.

Before capture the chunk is run three times on a side stream, warming the caching
allocator and any lazy kernel initialization. Captured graphs are cached on $G$,
$S$, $P$, the chunk length $K$, whether a prior term is enabled, the dtype and the
device. Only \emph{whether} there is a prior is selected on the device, because the prior
variance lives in the buffer set as a scalar tensor the replay reads, so one graph
serves datasets whose prior variances differ.

\paragraph{Triton full-loop fusion.}
\label{sec:triton}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/triton_dispersion_schematic.png}
\caption{The fused Triton dispersion kernel. One kernel handles both gene-wise
fits of the dispersion pipeline: the Cox--Reid MLE, and the MAP shrinkage with a
Gaussian prior that follows the trend fit (the trend fit itself runs outside
Triton). One Triton program is launched per gene; it loads $\mathbf{y}_g$,
$\boldsymbol{\mu}_g$ and the design $\mathbf{X}$ once, then runs every iteration
of the gradient ascent register-resident, with the NB log-likelihood, the
Cox--Reid term $-\tfrac12\log\det\mathbf{B}$ ($\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}$), the $\log\alpha$-gradient and the Armijo line search all
fused into the loop body. Only the converged $a_g=\log\alpha_g$ is written back
to global memory. Supplementary
Algorithms~\ref{alg:triton-overview}--\ref{alg:triton-detail} give the
pseudocode.}
\label{fig:triton}
\end{figure}

The Triton mode fuses a gene's entire fit into a single program, launched once per
gene (Fig.~\ref{fig:triton}). Its counts, $\mu$ and design columns are loaded once
into registers, and the gradient ascent then runs register-resident: the
Cox--Reid $P\times P$ solve (closed-form $2\times2$ for $P{=}2$, an unrolled
no-pivot LU above that), the NB log-likelihood and $\log\alpha$-gradient
(\texttt{lgamma} and \texttt{log1p} from \texttt{libdevice}, \texttt{digamma}
hand-implemented to match ATen's \texttt{calc\_digamma} through a recurrence to
$x\ge10$ followed by the Bernoulli asymptotic series), and the Armijo line
search. A per-gene \texttt{while} loop lets each gene exit as soon as it
converges, so the kernel does not run every gene to the batch maximum. The kernel
is fp64 and covers $P\in\{2,\dots,6\}$; \texttt{fit\_dispersions} gates on the
covered range and routes wider designs to the eager path, while the kernel's own
launcher raises instead of fitting a truncated model, since it reads a fixed
number of design columns. At the gene-wise step, non-converged and
high-dispersion genes get the same noIncrease revert and grid fallback as the
eager path, computed eagerly after the kernel returns; at the MAP step neither
applies, in either mode, because R applies neither. The fused reduction order and
the hand-written \texttt{digamma} move the last bits, so this mode is not
bit-identical to eager, and Sec.~\ref{sec:parity} reports how far the two
diverge on real data.

Covering a range of $P$ needed one extra step. The eager path writes the $P\times
P$ elimination as a Python loop over tensor slices, but Triton's frontend admits no
local arrays and no compile-time sequence building, so a register-resident matrix
cannot be indexed by a loop variable: the algebra must appear as straight-line code
specialized to each $P$. Because that grows quadratically, from $20$ lines at
$P{=}2$ to $174$ at $P{=}6$, we emit it from a generator mirroring the eager
elimination operation for operation rather than hand-writing each case; only the
$P\in\{2,4\}$ arms are hand-written. Both kinds of arm are checked against the
eager Cox--Reid term at fixed $\alpha$, which isolates the linear algebra from the
optimizer's path: over all covered $P$ and $S\in\{8,12,24\}$ the log-posterior
agrees to \fact{3e-15} relative and its gradient to \fact{3e-14}, against committed
test bounds of 1e-13 and 1e-12.

\subsection{GLM acceleration}
\label{sec:glm}
The GLM is implemented as a batched negative-binomial IRLS solve on the GPU,
using one batched factorization across genes at each iteration. Genes that
diverge or reach the DESeq2 iteration cap use the same host-side fallback as the
reference. Wald standard errors retain the $\mu\ge0.5$ floor in
\texttt{fitBeta.cpp}; LRTs use the full--reduced deviance difference.

\subsection{LFC shrinkage acceleration}
\label{sec:apeglm}
apeGLM \citep{zhu2019apeglm} is a per-gene optimization, so its L-BFGS state,
line search, and convergence flags are batched on the GPU. We match the
reference optimizer's initialization and stopping rules because extreme,
low-count genes can have competing posterior modes. As in \texttt{lfcShrink},
only the shrunken coefficient and its standard error are replaced; p-values
remain those from the Wald fit.

\subsection{Validation methodology}
\label{sec:parity-method}
Reference fixtures are generated by \texttt{scripts/generate\_r\_fixtures.R}
against R~DESeq2~1.52.0: three single-factor designs at $12\times200$,
$30\times500$ and $60\times2000$ (samples $\times$ genes, $P=2$), a
$60\times1500$ two-factor design $\sim$\,batch\,$+$\,condition with a
three-level batch ($P=4$), and a $60\times1000$ continuous-covariate design
($P=2$). Every intermediate is checked against R: size factors,
\texttt{dispGeneEst}, \texttt{dispFit} (the parametric trend on every fixture,
plus the \texttt{"local"} and \texttt{"mean"} trends on the single-factor
ones), \texttt{dispMAP}, the final dispersion and its outlier set, $\beta$,
$\mu$, the hat-matrix diagonals, Cook's distances, Wald SE, the LRT statistic on
all five designs, the independent-filtering \texttt{padj}, and the apeGLM prior
scale and shrunk LFC/SE. Errors are reported as p95 relative values, or absolute
values where a quantity crosses zero. Sec.~\ref{sec:parity} reports the measured
agreement for the principal quantities and tabulates the five pipeline stages on
the real datasets (Table~\ref{tab:parity}).

\section{Results}

\subsection{Experimental setup}
All GPU measurements are taken on one NVIDIA A100-SXM4-40GB under PyTorch~2.6
with CUDA~12.4 and NVIDIA driver~550.90.07. The reference CPU baseline
(host~A) is R~DESeq2~1.52.0 on a
12-core box, with R~4.6.0 and apeglm~1.34.0. Every timing is a warm median of $N$
repetitions with \texttt{torch.cuda.synchronize()} on both sides of the timed
region, so no GPU work is left in flight when the clock stops. End-to-end R
timings use both one worker and 12 BiocParallel workers; Table~\ref{tab:realtiming}
uses the faster configuration for each design.

We validate on three real, published RNA-seq datasets sliced into six designs
spanning $P=2$--$5$ model terms: pasilla \citep{brooks2011pasilla}
(\emph{Drosophila}, $n{=}7$, one- and two-factor), airway
\citep{himes2014airway} (human, $n{=}8$, three design slices), and a 300-sample
GTEx \citep{gtex2020atlas} whole-blood vs.\ skeletal-muscle contrast
($54{,}922$ genes). The first two are distributed as Bioconductor experiment
packages; the GTEx counts are recount2's uniform reprocessing of SRA study
SRP012682 \citep{colladotorres2017recount2}, from which we draw 150 samples per
tissue at a fixed seed and convert base coverage to read counts by dividing by
the average read length. Scaling studies use fixed-seed negative-binomial
simulations where the gene and sample axes can be swept independently.

\subsection{Numerical parity with R}
\label{sec:parity}
\begin{sloppypar}
Correctness is checked per substep against R on all six real datasets
(Table~\ref{tab:parity}). Synthetic fixtures cover three single-factor designs,
a multi-factor design, and a continuous covariate. On these fixtures, size
factors agree to $<$1e-10, gene-wise dispersion to $\sim$1e-9, and MAP
dispersion, $\beta$, $\mu$, the Hessian and Wald SEs to $\sim$1e-6 relative. A further suite
(\texttt{tests/test\_triton\_dispersion.py}) pins the fused kernel against the
eager path once per covered $P$, the axis along which its specialized arms
(Sec.~\ref{sec:triton}) could diverge without any other test noticing.
\end{sloppypar}

All 30 substep checks pass, and every one of the six dispersion comparisons is
within \fact{0.44\%} of R, the worst being \texttt{gtex\_blood\_muscle} at
\fact{4.4e-3}. The small-degrees-of-freedom case is not the outlier one might
expect. R enters the simulation-based prior-variance estimator only when the
residual degrees of freedom satisfy $0<m-p\le3$, so \texttt{airway} ($n{=}8$,
$P{=}5$, dof~3) is the only one of the six datasets to take that branch at all;
because the branch reproduces R's scalar bit for bit (Sec.~\ref{sec:disp}), its
dispersion agrees to \fact{4.5e-4}, its raw LFC to \fact{3.0e-5}, and its
significant-gene set matches R's \emph{exactly} (Jaccard \fact{1.000}). The
apeGLM shrinkage matches R to Pearson $r\ge0.997$ on every dataset, with
p95 $|\Delta|\le$3.4e-3. The residual is confined to flat, near-degenerate genes
on which two limited-memory-BFGS implementations break a tie between
equal-posterior optima differently; the shrunk LFC is a reported effect size and
does not feed the significance calculation, so it changes no call. Across the
\fact{25} synthetic dispersion configurations of Sec.~\ref{sec:roofline} and
Sec.~\ref{sec:ablations}, the graph path reproduces eager exactly: gene-wise, MAP
and final dispersion all agree to max $|\Delta|=\fact{0}$
(\texttt{benchmarks/results\_a100*.json}). Triton's fused reduction order and
hand-written \texttt{digamma} change the last bits; its dispersion agrees with
eager to $\le$3.2e-7 on the five small-$n$ sets. The standard-pipeline
\texttt{gtex} comparison includes count replacement and refitting in every
mode (Table~\ref{tab:realtiming}).

\subsubsection{Parity in the standard DE plots}
\label{sec:deplots}
The residuals above are summary statistics, but a DESeq2 run is read through a
small set of standard diagnostic plots, and parity is only useful if it survives
into those. Fig.~\ref{fig:deplots} redraws the three an analyst looks at
first (the dispersion-estimate, MA and volcano plots) from both pipelines by
identical code, on axis limits taken from R and reused for \cudeseq{} so the rows
are compared rather than each rescaled to its own data. They are visually
indistinguishable: the gene-wise MLE cloud, the fitted trend, the shrunken MAP
band and the \fact{141} genes the outlier rule exempts from shrinkage all land in
the same places, and the same \fact{3{,}993} genes are called at
$\mathrm{padj}<0.05$.

Fig.~\ref{fig:concordance} quantifies the same agreement across all six designs in
the terms a DE analysis is used for. The two pipelines put the same genes at the
top of the ranked list: at every $N$ up to 2{,}000 and on every design they share
\fact{$\ge$99.6\%} of the top $N$, and their called sets agree to a Jaccard index
of \fact{$\ge$0.961} across the whole range $\alpha\in[10^{-3},0.2]$. At
$\alpha{=}0.05$, four of the six agree exactly and the other two reach
\fact{0.99963} and \fact{0.99997}. Ranking uses $|$Wald
statistic$|$ rather than the p-value because on \texttt{gtex} R reports $p=0$ for
hundreds of underflowed genes, so a p-value ranking would compare arbitrary
tie-breaks among them.

The first is a $\mu$ floor in the Wald standard error. R forms the weights for its
coefficient covariance from a $\mu$ already thresholded at \texttt{minmu}
(\texttt{fitBeta.cpp}), whereas our IRLS returns $\mu$ unthresholded because
Cook's distance requires it raw; omitting the floor when forming
$X^{\!\top}\!WX$ for the SE drops any sample with $\mu<0.5$ out of the weight
matrix and inflates the SE. The effect is invisible on ordinary genes and severe
on degenerate ones. Without the floor the SE runs \fact{22\%} high on genes where
one contrasted group is \emph{entirely} zero, which is enough to change
\texttt{airway\_cell}'s called set and pull its Jaccard index against R's
\fact{206} genes to \fact{0.971}, and $\sim$7\% high on near-zero-count genes
whose dispersion is pinned at the ceiling, moving their raw p-values by up to
\fact{0.07}. With the floor applied the SE matches R to \fact{0.03\%} on those
same degenerate genes, \texttt{airway\_cell} and \texttt{airway} both reproduce
R's called set \emph{exactly}, and the histograms of panel~(a) coincide. Only the
few genes carrying $\mu<0.5$ in some sample are affected at all, which is why
every aggregate metric in Table~\ref{tab:parity} is essentially unchanged either
way. A suite scoring only aggregates can miss a detail of this kind.

The standard wrapper has an additional branch when a design cell contains at
least \texttt{minReplicatesForReplace}$=7$ samples. Both implementations flag
counts above the 0.99 quantile of the Cook's-distance $F$ distribution, replace
eligible counts with the 20\%-trimmed mean of normalized counts rescaled by the
sample size factor, and refit the affected genes. The refit re-estimates the
gene-wise dispersion while retaining the original fitted trend and dispersion
prior, then repeats the MAP and Wald fits. Original counts are retained for
subsequent shrinkage. This branch is unreachable for the five seven- or
eight-sample designs; it is exercised by \texttt{gtex}, with 150 samples in each
group. Table~\ref{tab:parity} therefore tests the same standard wrapper path for
both implementations rather than comparing \cudeseq{} with
\texttt{nbinomWaldTest} alone.

\subsection{Scaling on synthetic data}
\label{sec:roofline}
Synthetic data lets the gene and sample axes move independently, which is what
identifies \emph{why} the dispersion loop is slow rather than only how slow it is.
We first establish the bound. Hardware counters were unavailable on this instance,
so the roofline is measured empirically: a three-array fp64 streaming probe sustains
\fact{1369\,GB/s}, or \fact{88\%} of the A100's 1555\,GB/s specified peak, and the
loop's kernels come nowhere near it. At $60\times20{,}000$ the elementwise weight
update reaches \fact{21\%} of sustained bandwidth and the \texttt{lgamma} and
\texttt{digamma} evaluations \fact{43\%} and \fact{36\%}; at $60\times2{,}000$ all
three fall to \fact{3--8\%}, compute-bound on the special functions rather than
starved for bandwidth. Neither regime is the binding constraint. What settles it is
the assembled objective-and-gradient evaluation \texttt{\_lp\_and\_dlp}, the body of
the loop: \fact{1287\,\textmu s} at $20{,}000$ genes and \fact{1292\,\textmu s} at
$2{,}000$. A tenfold change in arithmetic and bytes moved produces no change in wall
time. This is the signature of a loop paying mainly for launches and dispatch.
That is the cost graph replay and Triton fusion attack, and why they buy more than a
better-tuned kernel could.

The synthetic scaling experiments below isolate the dispersion optimizer rather
than report end-to-end DESeq2 speedups; they therefore do not exercise the
standard replacement/refit wrapper branch. The real-data timings in
Table~\ref{tab:realtiming} use that branch where applicable.
If the loop is launch-bound, the accelerators should pay most where iterations are
many and each is cheap, and fade as every iteration acquires enough arithmetic to
fill the device. The sample axis tests that directly
(Table~\ref{tab:samplesweep}). Holding genes at $2{,}000$ and sweeping $S$ from a
$2\mathrm{v}2$ pilot to a $2{,}000$-sample cohort, the graph's speedup on the
isolated gene-wise ascent loop declines from \fact{7.6$\times$} at $S{=}4$ to
\fact{0.96$\times$} at $S{=}2000$: by two thousand samples the launch overhead it
removes is a rounding error against the per-iteration work, and replay is no longer
worth its bookkeeping. Triton decays over the same range but never loses, from
\fact{69.0$\times$} to \fact{7.7$\times$}, since fusion removes the intermediate
traffic as well as the launches. Two features of the sweep are structural rather
than noise. The $S{=}4$ and $S{=}6$ rows run the full \fact{100}-iteration cap
while every larger $S$ converges in \fact{13--25}, which is most of why the
small-sample regime is both the slowest and the most accelerable. And $S{=}4$ is
the one row where the stage-level speedup ($1.8\times$) falls far below the
loop-level one ($69.0\times$): with $m-p=2$ that design takes the stochastic KL
branch (Sec.~\ref{sec:disp}), whose compiled replay of R's random stream is serial
host work no GPU mode touches, contributing $\approx$\fact{470\,ms} to every mode
at $S{=}4$ and nothing at $S{=}6$. It is Amdahl's remainder for that row.

The gene axis says the same thing from the other side. Taking $2{,}000$ to
$20{,}000$ genes at fixed $S{=}60$ multiplies the work tenfold but the eager stage
time only by \fact{1.7$\times$} at $P{=}2$ (\fact{127}$\to$\fact{210}\,ms) and
\fact{1.2$\times$} at $P{=}4$ (\fact{193}$\to$\fact{224}\,ms): nine tenths of the
added genes are absorbed into launch latency the small case already paid. Because
that fixed overhead is amortized over more genes at $20{,}000$, it is also where
the graph has least left to remove, its stage speedup dropping from
\fact{4.4$\times$} to \fact{1.7$\times$} at $P{=}2$ (\fact{3.7$\times$} to
\fact{2.2$\times$} at $P{=}4$). Graph capture is a warm-path optimization: the
one-time capture costs \fact{487--796\,ms} across these four configurations, so it
is recovered only when many matrices of one shape are fitted in a session, as in the
serving pattern the virtual-cell evaluation loops of the Introduction generate.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:ablations}
Three design choices in Sec.~\ref{sec:modes}--\ref{sec:apeglm} could reasonably
have gone the other way, and each is worth separating from the headline numbers.

\textbf{Chunk length, and why a full-length capture fails.} The obvious way to
graph an iterative loop is to capture all of it, and that is the worst
configuration we measured. Because a captured graph cannot branch on a convergence
flag, a capture of $k$ iterations must be replayed $\lceil \mathrm{iters}/k \rceil$
times, so the loop executes $\lceil \mathrm{iters}/k \rceil \cdot k$ iterations
rather than the $\mathrm{iters}$ eager early-exits after; done genes are frozen
no-ops but still cost their share of the kernel. Sweeping $k$ over the divisors of
$\mathrm{maxit}=100$ (Table~\ref{tab:chunksweep}) traces that cost model. The
full-length capture $k{=}100$ rounds $26$ real iterations up to $100$ and gives
back almost all the benefit: \fact{1.83$\times$} instead of \fact{6.01$\times$} at
$2{,}000$ genes, \fact{0.99$\times$} instead of \fact{2.42$\times$} at $20{,}000$,
where it is indistinguishable from not graphing at all. What the sweep shows is
that the padded iteration count, not the replay count, sets the time: rows
executing the same number of iterations land within a couple of per cent of each
other however many replays that took ($k{=}25$ and $k{=}50$ both pad to $50$ and
differ by $0.4\%$ at $2{,}000$ genes; $k{=}2$ through $k{=}20$ all pad to $40$ and
differ by $2\%$ at $20{,}000$). At $2{,}000$ genes $k{=}2$ is fastest
(\fact{6.01$\times$}) because it avoids padding, whereas at $20{,}000$ genes
$k{=}10$ and $k{=}20$ tie at \fact{2.42$\times$}. The current $k{=}10$ default
is therefore a conservative compromise rather than a universal optimum.

\textbf{Per-gene early exit in the fused kernel.} The Triton kernel's loop is
guarded per program by \texttt{(it < maxit) \& (\textasciitilde done)}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:triton}) rather than run for a fixed trip count, and the iteration
counts of Table~\ref{tab:samplesweep} bound what that is worth: outside the
small-sample regime the loop converges in \fact{13--25} iterations against a cap
of \fact{100}, so a fixed-trip kernel would execute four to nearly eight times
the loop body for an identical answer. The saving is largest exactly where the
batch is widest, since a fixed trip count makes every gene wait for the cap
rather than for its own convergence. This is a bound inferred from the shipped
kernel's measured iteration counts rather than a measured A/B: the released code
exposes no fixed-trip switch to compare against.

\textbf{The apeGLM optimizer is a parity choice, not a speed choice.} A batched
Newton solver for the shrinkage step is both simpler to write and faster than
porting apeGLM's L-BFGS, and it disagrees with R. The
reason is the one described in Sec.~\ref{sec:apeglm}: the Cauchy-prior posterior
is bimodal for extreme-effect genes, R reports whichever mode its own optimizer
reaches from its own initialization, and a solver that finds the global optimum
therefore returns a better estimate that is not the reference's. This is the one
place where improving on the reference and reproducing it are in direct conflict,
and a drop-in replacement has to choose reproduction. Porting the reference's
optimizer restores agreement to $r\ge$\fact{0.997} on shrunk log-fold-changes
across all six designs (Table~\ref{tab:parity}) while keeping the stage on the
GPU with no per-gene host fallback.

\section{Discussion}
\cudeseq{} shows that the standard DESeq2 analysis pipeline can be moved to a GPU
without replacing its statistical model or sacrificing quality.
Across six real-data designs, the implementation
reproduced the intermediate quantities used by normalization, dispersion
estimation, GLM fitting, significance testing, and LFC shrinkage
(Table~\ref{tab:parity}), while reducing end-to-end runtime by
\fact{3.1--13.8$\times$} relative to the fastest
R~DESeq2 run on the A100 benchmark host.
These gains make repeated, reference-compatible differential-expression
analyses more practical in settings such as model evaluation, where the same
analysis is run many times.

The primary speedup comes from replacing sequential per-gene optimization with batched
GPU computation.  We obtain further speedup through 
CUDA-graph replay and Triton fusion which reduce the
overhead of the dispersion iterations. 
Speedup varies across designs due to factors such as: the time spent in dispersion fitting, 
shrinkage, and the remaining CPU steps depends on sample size and the design matrix.

We note that our validated scope includes single-factor designs with up to four levels,
additive multi-factor designs with up to $P{=}5$, and continuous covariates. 
The fused Triton path supports
$P\in\{2,\dots,6\}$ and otherwise uses the eager implementation. 

We note that in many instances numerical equivalence depends on subtle
implementation details beyond published technical specifications. 
The dispersion prior, Wald
standard errors, and apeGLM shrinkage each exposed behavior that is not fixed
by the model specification alone (Sections~\ref{sec:disp},
\ref{sec:deplots}, and~\ref{sec:apeglm}). Reproducing such details is sometimes
necessary to obtain DESeq2 equivalent output. 
For example, we applied DESeq2’s minmu floor when forming the
GLM weight matrix used to calculate Wald standard errors; without it, 
genes with near-zero fitted counts receive different standard errors 
and can produce different p-values.

In other instances we achieve fidelity to the deseq2 method descrption
with minor differences in floating-point rounding.
For example Triton evaluates the same dispersion objective 
in a fused kernel, but its different floating-point reduction order 
and digamma implementation introduce small numerical differences from 
eager PyTorch. On GTEx, the dispersion difference was $1.3 \times 10^{-4}$
at the 95th percentile and at most $1.69 \times 10^{-2}$ for any gene; 
across the five smaller designs, it was at most $3.2 \times 10^{-7}$. 
In such instances, it is worth noting that these differences arise from
implementation choices, not from a change to the DESeq2 model, 
and neither implementation is inherently closer to the intended calculation
under that statistical model specification.

In conclusion, cuDESeq2 preserves the DESeq2 analysis while making
repeated differential-expression analyses substantially faster on a GPU.
Our hope is that this improvement will support the bioinformatics community by enabling
reference-compatible DE analysis in workflows that require many repeated comparisons.
